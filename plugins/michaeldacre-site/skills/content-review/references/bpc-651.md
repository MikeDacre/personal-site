# California Business & Professions Code § 651 — reference

Detail behind Check 3 of the `content-review` skill. Read when a finding is borderline and
the summary in SKILL.md is not enough to make the call.

**This is a content-review aid, not legal advice.** § 651 governs Mike's licence. Anything
genuinely ambiguous goes to him, and where money or a formal claim is involved, to a
lawyer.

## Scope

§ 651 applies to any person licensed under Division 2 of the Business & Professions Code —
which includes physicians — and covers advertising disseminated to induce people to seek
their services. A personal website that describes a physician's practice and invites
contact is advertising under this section. The fact that it is a personal site rather than
a clinic's marketing site does not exempt it.

## What the statute prohibits

The core prohibition is any **"false, fraudulent, misleading, or deceptive statement,
claim, or image"**. In practice that covers:

### Misrepresentation and omission

- Misrepresenting facts, or concealing material information.
- Statements that are literally true but create an unjustified expectation of results.
- Endorsements that omit material facts.

### Images

- Altered images, or photographs that do not accurately depict actual results.
- Images of models presented without clear disclosure that they are models rather than
  patients. *Practical implication: any stock photography of "patients" needs disclosure,
  which is a strong reason to avoid stock people entirely on this site.*
- Before/after photographs without specifying the procedures performed and noting that
  results vary.

### Claims

- Unsubstantiated claims of superiority or of professional competence. This is the one
  most likely to appear accidentally in ordinary warm prose — "leading", "top", "best in
  the area", "unmatched", "renowned".
- Scientific claims not supported by reliable, peer-reviewed, published scientific
  studies. Relevant to Mike's stated interests in integrative and trauma-informed
  practice, where the literature is uneven: describing an *approach* is fine, asserting an
  *outcome* needs support.
- Guarantees of results, explicit or implied.

### Testimonials and endorsements

Any statement, endorsement, or testimonial must disclose material facts or it risks
violating the section. Combined with the prohibition on creating unjustified expectations,
the workable position for this site is simple: **no testimonials at all.** Not favourable
ones, not unsolicited ones, not paraphrased ones, not screenshots of reviews.

This is stricter than the statute strictly requires. It is the right default because the
compliance cost of getting a testimonial right exceeds any benefit it brings to a personal
site whose job is to convey how Mike practises.

## Board certification — specific rules

Physicians and podiatrists face explicit constraints:

- Only certifying boards recognised as members of the American Board of Medical
  Specialties, or an equivalent approved body, qualify.
- Advertising "board certified" requires the **full name of the certifying board**,
  displayed with **comparable prominence** to the claim itself.
- Certifications from non-qualifying bodies may not use "board certified" terminology at
  all.

**Practical rule for review:** wherever "board certified" appears, the board's full name
must appear adjacent and at similar visual weight. "Board certified in Family Medicine" is
not sufficient on its own; "Board certified by the American Board of Family Medicine" is
the shape to look for. Flag any instance where the phrase appears without the board named,
or where the board name is visually subordinated — smaller type, a footnote, a tooltip.

Also flag any description of Mike as a specialist in a field he is not certified in.
Describing clinical *interests* is fine; implying credentialed specialty status is not.

## Fee and pricing disclosures

If fees are advertised, § 651 requires specific disclosures: exact pricing rather than
"as low as" framing, all variables that affect cost, which insurance plans are accepted,
and disclosure of compensation paid to media representatives.

**Review position:** do not attempt to make fee content compliant. Flag it and route it to
Mike. Fee advertising is where the disclosure requirements are most detailed and the
downside of getting it wrong is highest.

## Review heuristics

When judging a borderline passage, three questions usually settle it:

1. **Would a reasonable prospective patient form a specific expectation of outcome from
   this sentence?** If yes, it needs either support or softening.
2. **Is this a claim about Mike relative to other physicians?** Comparative framing is
   almost always the problem, even when phrased modestly.
3. **Could this be read as a promise?** Future-tense statements about how a patient will
   feel are the common trap.

## Phrasing that is fine

The statute constrains claims, not warmth. All of these are compatible with § 651 and with
the site's patient-facing goals:

- Describing how Mike approaches care, what a visit is like, what he pays attention to.
- Stating training, credentials, and current role factually.
- Describing clinical interests, as interests.
- Explaining a philosophy of practice — integrative, trauma-informed, whole-person — as
  an approach rather than a guaranteed result.
- Plain statements of what services are offered.

The goal of this review is not to strip the warmth out of the site. It is to keep the
warmth in the description of *how Mike works* rather than in claims about *what patients
will get*.
